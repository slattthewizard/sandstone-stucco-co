## Why stucco cracks at all

Stucco is a cement-based coating, and cement-based coatings crack. Imasco, a manufacturer of cement stucco, states that "some cracking and efflorescence are inherent to portland cement, and are not a reflection of product quality." That's a manufacturer's statement about its own product, and it's worth keeping in mind before you assume every line on the wall is a defect.

Calgary adds its own pressure. The City of Calgary says stucco does not handle moisture well, and that moisture and snow can cause stucco to shrink and crack. The City counted 117 freeze-thaw cycles in 2022, against a 30-year average of 128, and 54 chinook days against an average of 46. Those figures are in the City's [2022 climate hazards year in review](https://www.calgary.ca/content/dam/www/uep/esm/documents/climate-hazards-2022-year-in-review.pdf). A wall that gets warmed and chilled that often is being moved, a little, all winter.

Martini Stone Masonry, a Calgary contractor, describes the mechanism this way: water penetrates small cracks, freezes and expands. That is the firm's own account of freeze-thaw cracking, and it's a plausible reason a small crack can grow over several winters. The same firm says temperature movement opens gaps of 3 to 8 mm where stucco meets window and door frames. Calgary Parging, another Calgary contractor, says that over 30% of its residential repairs, going by its own 2024 jobs, address water damage caused by incorrectly installed gutters, eaves or flashing. So a crack isn't always the wall's fault. Sometimes it's where water has been sent.

## Hairline cracks: usually the cosmetic end

A hairline crack is thin enough that you can barely slide a fingernail into it. It may be straight, or it may run in a loose web across a section of wall. Given what Imasco says about portland cement, a fine crack that stays the same size is the least worrying kind.

That's not the same as ignorable. A hairline crack is still an opening in a coating that's meant to shed water. Calgary Parging describes stucco as "designed to deflect water, not to be waterproof," and says the layer that actually keeps water out of the house is the building paper behind it. If that firm is right, a thin crack in the stucco matters less than whether the paper and flashing behind it are intact. You can't see them from outside, which is one reason a contractor's view on site counts for more than a photo.

Things that suggest a hairline crack is still just a hairline crack:

- It was there last year and looks the same.
- It's dry, with no staining, no dark streak running down from it and no flaking at the edges.
- The wall around it sounds solid when you tap it, and isn't soft or spongy.
- It doesn't line up with a window or door corner, a roofline or a deck connection.

None of these is a guarantee.

## Wider cracks: where the advice changes

High End Construction, a Calgary firm, says that "large cracks (over 1/4″ wide) or stucco that is crumbling off the wall are signs of deeper issues." That's one firm's rule of thumb, not a code or a standard, but it's a useful line to carry. A quarter inch is about 6 mm. If your crack is wider than that, stop treating it as cosmetic and get someone to look.

Width isn't the only thing to watch. These are the signs that make a crack more than a surface issue:

- The crack is getting wider or longer from one season to the next.
- The edges of the crack are uneven, with one side sitting proud of the other.
- Stucco near the crack is bulging, hollow-sounding or lifting away from the wall.
- You see stains, peeling paint, soft spots or mould-coloured marks nearby, inside or out.
- The crack runs from a corner of a window or door.

Weststar Wall Systems, a Calgary contractor, lists "soft or spongy areas" and "peeling or bubbling" among its warning signs for stucco damage. Total Exteriors, another Calgary firm, says to consider repair when damage is localized, moisture intrusion is minimal and hasn't spread, and the majority of the stucco is structurally sound. It says replacement may be the answer when damage is widespread or structural, or moisture problems are extensive. We cover what water damage looks like in more detail in [signs of stucco water damage](/blog/stucco-water-damage-signs/).

Calgary Stucco and Slate says, "Nobody can tell you the full extent of water damage until the wall is opened up." It's a fair warning. A crack can look the same whether it's a surface issue or the visible end of a wetter problem, and a contractor may not be able to say for sure until they've opened the wall.

## Cracks at windows and doors

Corners of openings are where cracks often show up first. Martini says temperature movement opens 3 to 8 mm gaps where stucco meets frames. That's more than a hairline, and it's usually a movement or sealing problem rather than a coating problem. We give window and door cracks their own page, so for the full picture see [stucco cracks around windows](/blog/stucco-cracks-around-windows/). The short version: a crack that starts at the corner of a window and heads diagonally away should be looked at, not just filled.

## Diagonal, stair-step and foundation cracks

If a crack in the stucco lines up with a crack lower down in the foundation or parging, or a crack runs diagonally from a corner, a stair-step pattern through block or brick, or keeps opening, the issue may not be the stucco at all. Stucco can only follow the wall behind it. If that wall is moving, no amount of patching the surface will change what's happening underneath.

That's a question for a qualified structural engineer. Sandstone Stucco Co does not offer foundation repair, waterproofing or structural assessment. A stucco contractor can tell you whether the coating is the problem, and an engineer is the person to say what's going on behind it. The same goes for water coming into a basement: it needs someone who deals with foundations. Our guide to [what parging is](/blog/what-is-parging/) explains why a crack in the coating at the base of the wall is a different thing from a crack in the foundation.

## Measure it, photograph it, watch it

Before you spend anything, spend a few minutes on a record.

1. Photograph the crack in good light, with a tape measure or a coin beside it, and note the date.
2. Measure the width at its widest point and the length, and write those numbers down.
3. Make a pencil mark at each end of the crack, so you can see whether it grows past them.
4. Repeat the photo and the measurement every couple of months, and again after the spring thaw.

A crack that hasn't moved in a year is a different problem from one that grew over a single winter. If it's moving, tell the contractor. If hail or another insured event might be behind it, the City of Calgary says to contact your home insurance provider before you do any repair work, and the Insurance Bureau of Canada says taking photos can be helpful. ## When to repair, and when to wait

Spring and summer are the usual repair season. Calgary Parging says stucco cures best above 5°C in dry conditions, typically April through October in Calgary, and that off-season repairs require heated hoarding. Imasco says to do no stucco work when air and substrate temperatures are below 4°C for 24 hours before, during and after application. So a crack you find in January is usually something to measure now and fix in spring.

In the meantime, a few sensible steps. Keep snow from piling against the wall; Martini says snow piled against stucco softens and crumbles the lowest 12 to 18 inches. Check that downspouts empty well away from the wall. Avoid pressure washing near a crack, since Weststar warns that high-pressure water can damage stucco and force moisture inside. And don't cover a crack with caulk or paint without knowing why it opened, because that can hide a problem rather than solve it.

If your home was built under a permit applied for on or after 1 February 2014, check your new home warranty before paying for repairs. Whether a given crack counts as a defect is for the warranty provider to decide. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are hairline cracks in stucco normal?

Imasco, a cement stucco manufacturer, states that some cracking is inherent to portland cement and not a reflection of product quality. A fine crack that stays the same size is often cosmetic. Even so, a contractor can tell you on site whether it's only in the coating.

### How wide is too wide for a stucco crack?

There's no official Calgary rule. High End Construction, a Calgary contractor, says cracks over 1/4 inch wide, or stucco crumbling off the wall, are signs of deeper issues. If a crack is wider than that, keeps growing or has bulging stucco nearby, have it looked at rather than waiting.

### Can I just seal a stucco crack myself?

A confident homeowner can sometimes seal a very fine, stable crack, but sealing hides it without saying why it opened. If the crack is moving, wet-stained or near a window or door, get a contractor to look first. A patch over a hidden moisture problem can trap water behind it.

### When should I call an engineer instead of a stucco contractor?

If a crack runs diagonally from a corner, follows a stair-step line through the wall, keeps opening, or lines up with a foundation crack, a qualified structural engineer is the right person to ask. Water coming into the basement is a foundation question too. A stucco contractor can tell you whether the stucco is the issue, but not what the foundation is doing.
