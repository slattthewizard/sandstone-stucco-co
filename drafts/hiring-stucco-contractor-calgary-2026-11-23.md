# How to Hire a Stucco Contractor in Calgary: A Vetting Checklist

Anyone can put "stucco specialist" on a truck door. Alberta doesn't have a trade ticket that tells you the person on your scaffold has passed a stucco exam, so the checking falls to you. ## Start with what Alberta says you can't rely on: a trade ticket

Plenty of home trades in Alberta require a certificate. Stucco isn't one of them. Alberta's Tradesecrets site lists the compulsory-certification trades, and it includes electricians, gasfitters, plumbers, sheet metal workers and welders among others. No plasterer, lather or stucco trade is on that list.

There is a related trade, Lather (Interior Systems Mechanic), but Tradesecrets lists it as optional certification. Its profile describes lathers as people who "install a wide variety of wall and ceiling systems and exterior finishes." Optional means a contractor may hold the ticket, but nobody has to in order to do the work.

So "Are they certified?" isn't a question with a clean answer for stucco. A contractor who says they're "certified" or "licensed" should be asked, politely, by whom and for what. What you can check is the list below.

## Check 1: the City of Calgary business licence

The City of Calgary says on its storm-damage page that contractors are required to have a valid City of Calgary business licence. It also tells homeowners to call 311 to confirm that the contractor has one.

Ask for the business name and licence number, then phone 311 and check it. It costs you a few minutes. Make sure the name on the licence matches the name on the quote, because a crew can work under a company name different from the one on the truck. A contractor who gets vague when you ask for it has told you something.

## Check 2: the prepaid contracting licence, where it applies

This one surprises people. Under Alberta's rules, a "prepaid contracting business" means soliciting, negotiating or concluding, in person and at any place other than the seller's place of business, a contract where all or part of the price is paid before the work is finished. Alberta.ca describes the covered work as "Construction, maintenance, repairing, altering, adding to or improving private dwellings, or real property used in conjunction with a private dwelling such as landscaping services."

Put plainly: a contractor who comes to your door or meets you at your house, agrees a job, and takes a deposit before finishing may need an Alberta prepaid contracting licence. The word "stucco" doesn't appear in the regulation, so whether your particular job falls inside it is a question for Service Alberta. Their licence search lets you look a business up. Check with Service Alberta rather than taking the contractor's word, or ours, for whether it applies.

The deposit side of this, including what the contract must say and your cancellation rights, gets its own page: [stucco contractor deposits in Alberta](/blog/stucco-contract-deposit-alberta/). It's worth reading before you hand anyone money.

## Check 3: Workers' Compensation Board coverage

Alberta.ca's advice on hiring a contractor includes this line: "Make sure the contractor and tradespeople have Workers' Compensation Board (WCB) coverage." Stucco work often means scaffolding, ladders and several people on site, so this isn't a formality.

Ask for the WCB account number or a clearance letter, and ask whether it covers the people who will be on your property, as well as the owner. Then confirm it with the WCB yourself. Alberta puts it on its short list of things to confirm, so it's worth the phone call.

## Check 4: more than one written estimate

Alberta.ca says to "Get written estimates from more than one business." Two or three is a sensible number for a repair, and more for a whole-house job.

Written matters. A figure given at the door, or a text that says "around three grand," isn't something you can compare line by line. When you have the estimates in front of you, check that they describe the same job. One quote may include scaffolding, colour matching and replacing damaged sheathing while another assumes none of them. The cheaper-looking number is only cheaper if it covers the same work. For how published prices vary by firm and what drives them, see the [stucco repair cost guide](/stucco-repair-cost-calgary/).

### Be wary of the door-knocker after a storm

Alberta.ca says "Most problems relating to home renovations involve contractors who solicit contracts door-to-door." That doesn't mean every contractor who knocks is a problem, but it's a reason to slow down, especially after a storm. Take the card, say you'll call back, and run the checks above before you agree to anything.

## What a written quote should name

A good quote is specific. For stucco, look for these items in writing:

- the business name, address and phone number, matching the City licence
- the area of wall covered, described so that you could point to it
- the materials to be used (the type of finish, base coat, mesh, paper or membrane if the work involves them)
- the work to be carried out, step by step: what gets removed, what gets repaired, what gets re-coated
- how the colour and texture will be matched, and whether that costs extra
- whether scaffolding, disposal and permits (if any) are in the price
- start date, expected duration and the weather conditions that would delay it
- the total price, whether GST is included, and when each payment is due

Alberta's rules point the same way for the kind of prepaid contracts covered above. For those worth more than $200, the Prepaid Contracting Business Licensing Regulation (s 10) says the contract must comply with s 35 of the Consumer Protection Act and set out the "quality or types of materials to be used under the contract and the services and work to be carried out." Even where that rule doesn't apply to your job, it's a fair description of what a quote should contain.

If you can't tell from the quote what's being done to your wall, ask. A contractor who can explain the job in plain words is easier to hold to it later.

## Check 5: your permit position

Most like-for-like stucco work doesn't need a City permit. The City of Calgary's storm-damage page says "For most repair or replacement work you will not need a development or building permit." It lists a building permit for siding in a *different* material, and development and building permits for larger or smaller windows or doors. Stucco isn't named on the page.

That means the safe step is to phone 311 about your own project and ask. If a contractor says no permit is needed, ask them to put in writing who confirmed it. If a permit is needed, the quote should say who applies for it.

## Check 6: new home warranty and insurance claims come first

Two things can change who you should call before a contractor.

If your home is newer, check your new home warranty before paying for repairs. The New Home Buyer Protection Act applies to homes built under a building permit applied for on or after 1 February 2014, and whether a given stucco problem counts as a defect is for the warranty provider to decide.

If hail or another insured event caused the damage, the City of Calgary advises: "Before you do any repair work on your property, contact your home insurance provider." Sandstone Stucco Co doesn't handle claims, but a contractor can give you a written estimate to pass to your insurer.

## Red flags worth taking seriously

None of these proves anything alone, but together they're a good reason to walk away:

- pressure to sign today, or a "price only good this week"
- a large deposit demanded up front, or payment requested with nothing in writing
- no written quote, or a quote that lists a total and nothing else
- reluctance to give a business licence number, WCB account or a street address
- a claim that you need a full re-stucco based on a quick look, with no explanation of what they saw. Ask what they found and where, and ask for photos
- a contractor who says they will "deal with your insurance company" for you. Adjusting a claim is regulated work in Alberta, and your insurer assigns its own adjuster

Alberta.ca's other short advice applies here too: always insist on a written contract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a stucco contractor need a trade certificate in Alberta?

Not a compulsory one. Alberta's Tradesecrets site does not list stucco or plastering among the compulsory-certification trades. Lather (Interior Systems Mechanic) is an optional-certification trade, so a contractor may hold that ticket but isn't required to. Check the business licence, WCB coverage and written contract instead.

### How do I check that a Calgary contractor has a business licence?

The City of Calgary says contractors are required to have a valid City of Calgary business licence, and that you can call 311 to confirm it. Have the business name and licence number ready. Make sure the name matches the one on the written quote.

### Should I get more than one stucco quote?

Yes. Alberta.ca advises getting written estimates from more than one business. Compare what each one actually covers, including materials, scaffolding, colour matching and any repairs to the wall underneath, since two totals can describe different jobs.

### Does the contractor need a prepaid contracting licence?

It depends on how the contract is made and paid. A business that solicits, negotiates or concludes a contract in person away from its own premises, where some or all of the price is paid before the work is finished, may need an Alberta prepaid contracting licence. Check with Service Alberta, which runs the licence search, for your job.
