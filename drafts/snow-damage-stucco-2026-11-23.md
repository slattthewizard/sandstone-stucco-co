# Snow Damage to Stucco: What Calgary Winters Do to the Base of a Wall

Look along the bottom of a stucco wall in March. On some houses the lowest foot or so looks fine. On others it's crumbly, stained or soft, and a piece comes off in your hand. Snow is often the first suspect, and a couple of Calgary sources treat it as a fair one. This page covers what they say, what Calgary's winters add to it, and what you can do about it before spring.

## What the sources say about snow and stucco

There's no single study to point at, so it helps to be clear about whose words these are.

The City of Calgary's climate-ready guidance on exterior walls and siding says that stucco "does not handle moisture well, and is not ideal for protection against heavy rain or winter storms." It adds that moisture and snow can cause stucco to shrink and crack. That's the City describing the material in general terms, and it's the closest thing to a neutral statement on the subject.

Martini Stone Masonry, a Calgary contractor, goes further and puts a number on it. The firm says snow piled against stucco softens and crumbles the lowest 12 to 18 inches of the wall. That's one contractor's claim from its own repair work, not a measured result.

Why would the base suffer most? The reasoning is simple, even if the exact figures belong to the contractors. Snow sitting against a wall stays wet for weeks. It melts in the afternoon, refreezes overnight, and keeps the lowest part of the wall damp while the rest of the wall dries off. A cement-based coating that's soaked again and again is a coating under stress.

## Freeze-thaw, and Calgary's version of it

Martini describes the general mechanism this way: water penetrates small cracks, freezes at -20°C and expands. The firm is describing stucco cracking, and the same logic applies wherever water can get into a hairline gap.

Calgary supplies plenty of chances for that cycle. The City counted 117 freeze-thaw cycles in 2022, which was below the 30-year average of 128. It also counted 54 chinook days, higher than the average of 46 chinook days per year. Those are the City's figures for that one year, from its [climate hazards year in review](https://www.calgary.ca/content/dam/www/uep/esm/documents/climate-hazards-2022-year-in-review.pdf), and they're a reminder that a Calgary winter is not one long freeze. A chinook can melt snow off a roof or drift in a day, and the water goes somewhere, often down the wall or into the base of it, before the temperature drops again.

None of this proves that your wall will fail. It does explain why a crack that looks harmless in October can look different in April.

## Where it shows up on a house

Snow damage tends to follow a few places on a house.

- At the base of the wall, where the stucco meets the parging or the foundation. This is where Martini's 12 to 18 inch band sits. If you're not sure where your stucco ends and parging begins, our explainer on [what parging is](/blog/what-is-parging/) covers the difference.
- Beneath downspouts and roof valleys, where a lot of melt water arrives in one spot.
- Along a driveway, walkway or deck, where snow gets shovelled or ploughed toward the house.
- Under windowsills and around openings, where cold and moisture meet a joint.

What you're looking for is a short list. Weststar Wall Systems, a Calgary-area contractor, names "soft or spongy areas" and "peeling or bubbling" as warning signs on stucco. Add any area that sounds hollow when you tap it lightly, any crack with a stain below it, and any bulge or loose edge. Hairline cracks on their own are common, and Imasco, a cement stucco manufacturer, states that some cracking and efflorescence are inherent to portland cement and not a reflection of product quality. The ones to watch are the ones that grow or sit where snow collects.

## Winter habits that help

Most of what helps is cheap and slightly tedious.

### Shovel back from the wall

When you clear snow, throw it away from the house, and leave a gap along the base of the wall if you can. A heap right against the stucco is the situation Martini describes.

### Don't pile driveway snow against the house

Ploughed or shovelled snow from a driveway or walk can end up in one long bank against a garage wall. If you have a favourite dumping spot, check that it isn't the foot of a stucco wall.

### Watch the downspouts

Check that they empty well away from the wall, and that extensions haven't been buried or knocked loose. Gutters and roof edges are a different trade from stucco, so if they need work, that's a separate job and a different contractor. Calgary Parging, a Calgary contractor, says over 30% of its residential repairs address water damage caused by incorrectly installed rain gutters, eaves or flashing, from its own 2024 jobs. That's one firm's mix of work, not a Calgary-wide figure, but it's a good reason to look up as well as down. Our post on [stucco leaks from gutters and flashing](/blog/stucco-gutter-flashing-leaks/) goes into that.

### Leave the ice alone

Don't chip ice off stucco with a shovel or metal tool, because you can take the surface with it.

### Skip the pressure washer in spring

It's tempting to blast off winter grime. Weststar warns that high-pressure water can damage stucco and force moisture inside, and Imasco's specification says not to spray walls with high-pressure water for at least 72 hours after application.

### Don't seal over a problem

A tube of caulk or a coat of paint over a soft patch can hide the damage and trap water behind it.

### Photograph the base in autumn and again in spring

A pair of dated photos of each wall is the easiest way to tell whether something changed over the winter. Put a coin or a tape measure beside any crack you're watching.

## When to have it looked at

Not every mark needs a call. A few do.

- The lowest part of the wall is soft, crumbling or coming off in pieces.
- A crack is widening, or you can see a stain, bubbling or flaking along it.
- A section sounds hollow, or a bulge has appeared.
- Water is visible behind or beneath the wall base, or you can smell damp inside along that wall.
- You're not sure whether the problem is the stucco, the parging or the foundation.

If you see any of those, take photos and get a written, no-obligation quote from an independent Calgary contractor. They can tell you on site whether the damage is just the coating or whether water has got further in. As Calgary Stucco and Slate puts it, nobody can tell you the full extent of water damage until the wall is opened up, so a first visit is about scope, not a final price.

If hail or another insured event may be involved, the City of Calgary advises contacting your home insurance provider before you do any repair work. And if your home is newer, check your new home warranty before paying for repairs. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does snow against my house damage stucco?

Martini Stone Masonry says snow piled against stucco softens and crumbles the lowest 12 to 18 inches of the wall. The City of Calgary says moisture and snow can cause stucco to shrink and crack. Both are general statements, so the condition of your own wall is what counts.

### How far should I keep snow from my stucco wall?

No source in our research gives a set distance. The sensible habit is to shovel snow away from the wall, not toward it, and to avoid building piles against the base. If a pile is unavoidable, check that area first when the snow melts.

### Can stucco be repaired in the middle of winter?

Manufacturers set temperature minimums for cement-based products: Imasco says no work below 4°C for 24 hours before, during and after application, and ADEX says at or above 5°C for at least 24 hours for its base coat. Calgary Parging says off-season repairs require heated hoarding. Ask any contractor how they'd keep the repair above those temperatures.

### How do I know if the damage is from snow or from something else?

You often can't tell from the ground. Gutter and flashing problems, hail and window gaps can all cause similar marks. A contractor can look at where the damage sits and where the water is coming from, and photographs taken over time help them see what's changed.
