## What Alberta calls a "prepaid contract"

The rules that matter here sit in the Designation of Trades and Businesses Regulation, which brings the prepaid contracting business under Part 10 of the Consumer Protection Act. It defines three connected terms.

A "construction or maintenance contract" is a contract for constructing, altering, maintaining, repairing, adding to or improving a building that the owner uses as their own private dwelling, or a structure used with that building on the same parcel. Business-use buildings, contracts between contractors and subtrades, warranty-covered new homes and water wells are excluded.

A "prepaid contract" is a construction or maintenance contract in which all or part of the contract price is to be paid before all the goods or services in the contract are provided. A deposit counts. So does paying half before the work starts.

A "prepaid contracting business" means the activities of soliciting, negotiating or concluding in person, at any place other than the seller's place of business, a prepaid contract.

The word "stucco" doesn't appear in that regulation. But Alberta.ca describes covered work as "Construction, maintenance, repairing, altering, adding to or improving private dwellings, or real property used in conjunction with a private dwelling such as landscaping services." Repairing the stucco on your own house sits comfortably inside that wording. Whether your particular job and your particular contractor are caught is a question for Service Alberta, not for an advertiser.

## When a contractor needs the prepaid contracting licence

The Consumer Protection Act, s 104(1), says: "No person may engage in a designated business unless the person holds a licence under this Act that authorizes the person to engage in that business." Prepaid contracting is a designated business.

Put the pieces together and the picture looks like this. A contractor who sells to you in person away from the contractor's own premises, such as at your home, and whose contract has money changing hands before the job is finished, is likely doing the thing the licence exists for. That's a reading of the regulation, not a ruling on any individual contractor.

Some situations fall outside the pattern. A contractor who meets you at their own shop or office is selling at a seller's place of business. A contract paid entirely on completion isn't a prepaid contract. Don't try to work out your case from a summary like this one. Phone Service Alberta and ask, then use its licence search to look the business up.

### What the licence involves

The Prepaid Contracting Business Licensing Regulation sets a licence fee of $78 (s 7(1)). It also requires the business to provide security "in a form and in an amount approved by the Director" (s 5(1)). We have not confirmed the amount of that security, and neither the regulation nor the Alberta.ca page states it, so we don't give a figure. Service Alberta can tell you what applies.

The same regulation limits unsolicited home sales for a short list of products: furnaces, air conditioners, water heaters, windows and energy audits, unless the homeowner invited the visit. Stucco and siding are not on that list, so a knock at the door from a stucco contractor isn't banned by that provision. That doesn't mean a door-knock is a good sign. Alberta.ca says "Most problems relating to home renovations involve contractors who solicit contracts door-to-door."

## What the contract has to say

For a prepaid contract of more than $200, the licensing regulation (s 10) requires the contract to comply with s 35 of the Consumer Protection Act and to set out the "quality or types of materials to be used under the contract and the services and work to be carried out."

For a stucco job, that translates into things you can point at on the page:

- the finish and base materials, and any membrane, paper or mesh going on
- what's being removed, repaired and re-coated, and on which walls
- the colour and texture match
- the price and when each payment is due

If your paper says only "stucco repair, $4,000, $2,000 deposit," it doesn't give you the detail the regulation describes. Ask for more before you sign. ## The 10-day cancellation right on direct sales

Part 2 of the Consumer Protection Act deals with "direct sales contracts." Under s 24(a.1), that is a consumer contract where the consideration exceeds an amount specified in the regulations and the contract is negotiated or concluded in person at a place other than the supplier's place of business. There are exceptions for markets and fairs.

For those contracts, s 27 says: "A consumer may, without any reason, cancel a direct sales contract at any time from the date the sales contract is entered into until, subject to the regulations, 10 days after the consumer receives a copy of the written sales contract."

Two points on that. First, the dollar threshold is set in the regulations, and we haven't confirmed what it is, so check with Service Alberta whether your contract exceeds it. Second, the clock starts when you receive a copy of the written contract, which is another reason to insist on one. A verbal agreement at the door, with no paper left behind, leaves the question of when the 10 days start unanswered.

If you want to cancel, don't rely on a phone call. Ask Service Alberta how to give notice and keep a dated copy of whatever you send.

## One year to cancel if the contractor needed a licence and didn't have one

Section 28(2)(a) of the Act gives a consumer a one-year cancellation right "if the supplier was required to be licensed under Part 10 and was not licensed at the time the direct sales contract was concluded."

That's the reason to check the licence before you pay, not after. If a contractor was required to hold a prepaid contracting licence and didn't, the Act gives you a longer window than the 10 days. Whether the licence was required in your case is again a matter for Service Alberta.

## A practical order of events

None of this is a guarantee of how any dispute turns out. As a sequence for a homeowner who is about to book a stucco job, though, it looks like this:

1. Get written estimates from more than one business, as Alberta.ca advises.
2. Before agreeing to anything, ask whether the contractor holds a prepaid contracting licence if they're selling at your home and want money up front. Look it up with Service Alberta.
3. Check the City of Calgary business licence by calling 311, and confirm WCB coverage.
4. Read the written contract. Does it name the materials and describe the work? Does it say when payments are due?
5. Keep your copy. The 10-day period runs from receiving it.
6. Ask Service Alberta if anything is unclear. That includes whether your contract is a direct sales contract and what the threshold is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for a stucco contractor to ask for a deposit?

A deposit request isn't a red flag on its own. In Alberta, paying before the work is finished makes the contract a "prepaid contract" if it covers work on your private dwelling, and the rules in this article may then apply. Ask for the terms in writing and check with Service Alberta.

### Does every stucco contractor need a prepaid contracting licence?

No, not automatically. The licence is tied to soliciting, negotiating or concluding a prepaid contract in person, away from the seller's own place of business. A contractor who meets you only at their own premises, or who is paid only after the work is finished, may fall outside it. Service Alberta can confirm for your job.

### Can I cancel a stucco contract I signed at my door?

You may be able to. Under s 27 of the Consumer Protection Act, a consumer may cancel a direct sales contract without any reason until, subject to the regulations, 10 days after receiving a copy of the written contract. The dollar threshold for a direct sales contract is set in regulation, so check with Service Alberta whether your contract qualifies.

### What if the contractor wasn't licensed when I signed?

Section 28(2)(a) gives a one-year cancellation right where the supplier was required to be licensed under Part 10 of the Act and wasn't licensed when the direct sales contract was concluded. Whether a licence was required is the key question. Check with Service Alberta, and keep your copy of the contract.
